*Part one of my [Lower Body Mobility](/lower-body-mobility/) series. Part two covers the [hips]({{< relref "posts/hips-that-will-not-fold" >}}).*

I have been squatting with a barbell plate under my heels for a while now. Without it I cannot even get into a bodyweight squat. Somewhere past halfway down, the front of my feet lifts, the part where the toes start, and I feel like I am about to fall over backwards.

I finally sat down and worked out why. This post is my notes, written for me, with the videos I am using so I do not do the drills wrong.

## What is actually happening

To stay upright in a squat, your centre of mass has to stay over the middle of your foot. As you go down, your hips travel back. Something has to travel forward to balance that, and the only thing that can is your knees and shins.

Shins moving forward over a flat foot means the ankle is closing. That movement is called **dorsiflexion**. A full-depth squat needs roughly 35 to 40 degrees of it. A lot of people, me included, have a fair bit less.

When the ankle runs out of range the body has two options. Either the heel lifts so the shin can keep going, or the shin stops and the hips keep going back. Mine does the second. The weight ends up behind my heel, the ball of the foot comes off the ground, and I tip.

The plate under the heel tilts the foot down at the front. That is pre-bent ankle. My shin can travel the same distance forward using less actual ankle movement, my knees come over my toes, and my weight lands back over the midfoot. Weightlifting shoes have a raised heel for exactly this reason.

So the plate is not cheating. It is the right setup for my ankles as they are today. But I would also like to need less of it.

## Where the weight should go

While I was at it, I also had the basic cue wrong. I was trying to "sit back on my heels", which pushes the hips further back and makes the tipping worse.

The better cue is a **tripod foot**: heel, base of the big toe, base of the little toe, all three pressed into the floor the whole way down. Drive through the whole foot with the heel glued down. If you can wiggle your toes at the bottom, the weight is in the right place.

And in a bodyweight squat the knees are supposed to go forward past the toes. That is normal, not a fault.

## Step 1: test the ankles

The quickest check is the **knee-to-wall test**. Barefoot, big toe about 4 inches from the wall, heel flat, push the knee straight forward to touch the wall. If you can, move the foot back an inch and try again. The furthest distance where the knee still touches with the heel down is your score.

How to read it. Standing flat, foot and shin sit at 90 degrees. Closing that angle is dorsiflexion, and the wall distance converts to degrees roughly like this:

| Toe-to-wall distance | Dorsiflexion | What it is enough for |
|---|---|---|
| 10 cm / 4 in or more | about 35 to 40 degrees | full-depth squat |
| 7 to 8 cm / 3 in | about 25 to 30 degrees | squat to parallel, then you tip |
| 5 cm / 2 in | about 20 degrees | clear restriction |

What you feel at the limit matters as much as the number:

- **A stretch in the calf** means the limit is muscle, mainly the soleus. That responds to the drills below.
- **A pinch or hard stop at the front of the ankle** with no stretch feeling points at the joint itself. Mobility work helps less here, and a heel lift is simply the long-term setup.

Test both sides. A big difference between left and right is worth fixing on its own.

Checking the angle by hand does not tell you much. Sitting with the leg relaxed, even a normal ankle only goes 10 to 20 degrees past square, because the calf is not loaded and the knee is usually straight. Standing with the knee bent and weight on the foot, the same ankle goes about twice as far. Squats are a bent-knee, loaded movement, so the standing number is the one that counts.

My result: **7 cm**, with a calf stretch at the end. So about 25 to 28 degrees, muscle-limited, and about 3 cm short of where I want to be. That lines up exactly with how my squat behaves: fine until roughly halfway, then the shin stops and I fall back.

## Step 2: the daily drills

Two things, about five minutes total, every day or at least before squatting.

### Knee-to-wall rocks

Same position as the test, done as reps instead of a one-off max:

- Set the foot about **1 cm closer than your max**. For me that is 6 cm. The knee should touch on most reps with some effort, not strain on every one.
- Drive the knee to the wall, hold one or two seconds, come back. That is one rep.
- **2 to 3 sets of 10 to 15 per side.** On the last few reps of a set, hold the knee at the wall for 5 to 10 seconds to turn it into a stretch.
- Heel stays down, knee goes straight at the wall, foot points straight. Same rules as the test, because those are what make the reps count.
- When every rep touches easily, move the foot back 1 cm.

A dumbbell or plate resting on the knee of the working leg, 5 to 10 kg, pushes the shin forward with less effort and tends to speed things up. Do it warm, daily is fine, and keep the cold max test separate: once every two weeks, find the true distance and write it down.

{{< youtube ElrpduJn92Y >}}

### Bent-knee calf stretch

This is the one I kept getting wrong. The usual straight-leg calf stretch hits the big calf muscle, the gastrocnemius. The muscle that limits squat depth is the deeper one, the **soleus**, and you only reach it with the knee bent.

Hands on the wall, one foot stepped back, back heel flat on the floor. Now **bend the back knee** and lean in. You should feel it low in the calf, near the Achilles. If you feel it high up behind the knee, your knee is still straight. Hold 30 to 60 seconds per side.

## Step 3: keep squatting, with the plate

No reason to stop training while the ankles catch up. Loaded squats with a heel lift are themselves one of the better mobility drills for this, as long as the knees travel forward, the whole foot stays pressed into the plate, and the chest stays up.

{{< youtube jlhMpsWL0kE >}}

Over the weeks the plan is to reduce the height of the lift. If it stops being needed, great. If it does not, that is also fine.

## If the ankles were never the problem

Some coaches argue that for a lot of people the limit is higher up, in the hips, and that no amount of calf stretching will move it. If I retest in three or four weeks and the knee-to-wall number has not changed, or the restriction feels like a pinch at the front of the ankle rather than a stretch in the calf, that is where I will look next:
